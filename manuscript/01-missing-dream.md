# Missing Dream

It all started as a dream—a dream that felt so real, so terrifying, and left behind the question of why you watch certain things.

After I woke up, I could not tell the difference—did I really watch a movie in a theater a couple of minutes ago, or was it just a dream?

The dream started in the morning with a school function. Maybe it was some kind of festive celebration. Students and teachers were chattering. A girl was sitting quietly in a corner. I don't know if she was scared. It felt like a late-1990s dream because she had a braid and wore a frock. I don't remember her face. In this story, I don't remember anyone's face clearly, just unclear details. I don't remember her face, but I remember the environment, the action, and the cinematic feeling so clearly.

The function started and ended. Then something terrible happened.

People started running, and so did she. I remember that she ran, and I ran beside her—it looked like it, but I could tell she didn't see me. Yes, I was beside her because I remember watching her run.

It was almost night. A silent road, a straight road, and then a corner.

Then she turned the corner. The girl didn't see the man yet, but *I* saw him. He was the man with red eyes, lying on the road. A soft road light reflected on his face. He was lying in front of a store, probably a beer shop, with many empty beer bottles around him.

As the little girl ran past him, his eyes opened, and he stood up to look at her. He didn't see me watching.

Then, almost like a movie flashback, another scene appeared. I only saw another man's back against a winter night, with a lamp behind him. He was talking with the drunken man about some money.

I didn't know what the money was for. I didn't know the reason behind it. But somehow, in that moment, I felt that this was connected to the girl. It felt like the drunken man was there because of her.

Then I saw the girl running again because she just wanted to reach her home. It was almost as if time had stopped, and I simply knew that she needed to run.

She came to a dark house and hid inside.

She didn't know what was going to happen. She didn't know the man had come to chase her. She was just hiding because it was winter, it was dark, and she couldn't find her home.

*I* was the one who was waiting for her to get away with this run. But as I watched, I didn't say any words to her. I felt no emotion. It looked like I was watching a movie with a missing part.

A very short, very loud sound, almost like a fire.

I don't know what happened next.

And then the dream ended with so many questions.

I woke up with a certain fog in my brain, and honestly, I didn't feel my heart racing or my breathing getting heavy at all. Then I heard a sharp doorbell. I needed to get up badly, probably because the newspaper had arrived.

I called out, “Coming,” and stood up from my bed.

I walked towards the door and picked up the newspaper. The first headline I saw was about a fire in an abandoned factory the previous night.

Then I heard my neighbour calling me.

“Kavya, Kavya.”

I turned around and said, “Good morning, Aunty. How’s your headache now?”

It was Sweeta Aunty, a woman in her forties who lived alone. She was the coolest person I had ever known. When I had shifted to Kolkata three years ago, she was one of the first people to welcome me, although her greeting had been quite threatening.

For me, she was a relationship advisor I had never asked for. But one thing about her was that her independence was quite motivating. She had a small café and a bookstore, and somehow managed to run both. She was sweet sometimes, scary sometimes, and yet always so energetic.

“My headache?” she said, touching her forehead. “Still there.”

“You should sleep properly.”

“Look who's giving me advice.”

“I’m not giving you advice.”

She looked at the newspaper in my hand.

“What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“No, I mean the newspaper.”

She took a quick look at the front page. Her expression changed slightly.

“Wait. This factory?”

I looked down at the headline again.

“What about it?”

“Isn’t this the factory near our colony?”

“I don't know, Aunty.”

“It is. I'm pretty sure it is.”

She started talking about the factory, about where it was and how long it had been abandoned. I listened, but honestly, I had very little interest in it.

She was unmarried and had very uncertain thoughts about men. She was also very passionate about my career as a psychologist and the difference between a psychologist and a doctor. I had explained the difference to her so many times that I had stopped counting.

I looked at the time.

“Aunty, I need to go to the clinic, so bye.”

I went inside and shut the door.

I put the newspaper on the table beside the door, just a little away from my dining table. It was a decent four-chair table, with a simple bowl of fruit sitting on it.

I sat at the table for a second. The fog in my mind was still there.

Then I looked at the newspaper again.

The factory.

For a second, something came into my mind—a fire, and the sound of it. Just a flash.

My dreams are actually so vivid that I know when I am dreaming, but I don't even remember what I watched afterward. Great. Being a psychologist.

My eyes moved to the clock.

8 a.m.

God, I needed to go to the clinic.

I usually liked slow mornings. I don't know why I had woken up this late today. I was usually a 6 a.m. person. But I knew there were some days when I stayed up until 1 a.m., especially when I was reading a book. Last night, I had been reading a short story, and somehow, after that, I had fallen asleep.

I got up and went to the kitchen.

The kitchen was right in front of the dining table. It was an open kitchen, and there was a window through which I could see Sweeta Aunty's bookstore-café. She was opening her café.

That type of person—energetic all the time. Sometimes I was jealous of her.

I waved at her through the window.

She waved back.

I made myself a cup of tea, without sugar, and then called home.

Mom picked up.

“Finally.”

“Good morning to you too, Ma.”

“Don't ‘good morning’ me. Where were you last night?”

“I was sleeping.”

“After what time?”

“Late.”

“How late?”

I smiled. “Why are you investigating me?”

“Because you didn't call me.”

“I was busy.”

“Busy enough to forget your mother?”

“Yeah. I was with someone.”

There was a pause.

“With someone?”

“A ghost.”

“Oh, very funny.”

I laughed. “Come on, Ma. I got late. That's all.”

“You could have sent one message.”

“I know.”

“One message.”

“I know, Ma.”

“Then why didn't you?”

“I forgot.”

“Exactly. You always forget.”

“You're keeping a record now?”

“I don't need a record. I have you.”

I laughed again.

“Okay, okay. I'm sorry.”

“Hmm.”

“Are you still angry?”

“No.”

“You sound angry.”

“I'm not angry.”

“You are definitely angry.”

“I'm talking normally.”

“This is your angry-normal voice.”

She laughed despite herself.

“Anyway, how are you feeling now?”

“I'm fine. How are you?”

“I'm fine. How's Papa?”

“He's fine. He went out for a while.”

“And Rishab?”

“College.”

“Studying?”

“He says so.”

I paused.

“That means no.”

“Probably,” Ma said.

I smiled.

“I’ll call him later.”

“You do that.”

“I will. And if he’s not studying, tell him I’m going to call.”

Ma laughed softly. “Why do you always threaten him?”

“Because someone has to.”

“He's studying.”

“Ma, you said the same thing last time.”

“He is.”

“Okay. I’ll believe you for now.”

“You’re too strict with your brother.”

“Someone has to be.”

“You already said that.”

“Because it’s true.”

She laughed.

“I’ll talk to him later.”

“Fine. But don't scare him.”

“I’m not going to scare him.”

“You are.”

“I’m just going to ask what he studied today.”

“That is scary enough for him.”

I laughed.

“Oh, and Ma?”

“What?”

“Check your account.”

“Why?”

“I sent you some money.”

“Kavya, why?”

“You said you wanted to buy something for yourself, didn't you?”

“I was just telling you.”

“Now you can actually buy it.”

“You don't have to send me money every time I say something.”

“I know.”

“Then?”

“I wanted to.”

She went quiet for a moment.

“Okay. Thank you.”

“Don't thank me. Go buy something nice.”

“Now you're ordering me around?”

“Yes.”

“Very good. Becoming your mother now?”

“Someone has to.”

She laughed.

I looked at the clock.

“Okay, Ma… I really have to go. I’m getting late for the clinic.”

“Hmm. Fine. Go.”

“I’ll call you tonight.”

“You better.”

“I will.”

“Okay.”

“Okay, Ma. Take care.”

“You too.”

There was a small pause.

“Love you.”

She smiled quietly on the other end. I could almost picture that little smile.

“Take care, beta.”

Her voice sounded a little softer than before.

“Okay, Ma.”

“Go now. You’re already late.”

I smiled.

“Bye.”

“Bye.”

I ended the call and looked at the time again.

I really had to go to the clinic.
